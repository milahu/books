Yes - and your specific goal changes the recommendations quite a bit.

You **don't really need a book about making realistic art**. For a *South Park*-like style, you're ultimately trying to learn:

- **shape simplification**
- **line control**
- **basic perspective / form**
- **gesture and posing**
- **character construction**
- **expressions**
- **visual abstraction**
- **composition**
- eventually **flat color and clean silhouettes**

The trick is to learn the fundamentals *underneath* cartooning, without getting trapped in photorealistic drawing.

#### 1. **Fun with a Pencil - Andrew Loomis**

**Probably the most interesting one for your particular goal.**

Loomis teaches drawing using extremely simple constructions - spheres, boxes, cylinders, etc. What's particularly relevant is that he uses those constructions to create **cartoon heads, characters and caricatures**, rather than treating cartooning as something completely separate from drawing fundamentals.

There are also exercises involving exaggeration and stylization.

For your purposes, I'd pay particular attention to the idea:

> **complex thing → simple geometric construction → stylized drawing**

That's basically the foundation of the kind of concept-to-cartoon workflow you're describing.

It's an older book, so don't expect a modern polished art textbook. But the underlying approach is excellent.

#### 2. **Modern Cartooning - Christopher Hart**

This is probably the most directly aligned with your intended end product.

It's explicitly aimed at **beginner cartoonists** and uses lots of step-by-step illustrations, character construction, expressions and cartoon design. ([PenguinRandomhouse.com](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/214645/modern-cartooning-by-christopher-hart/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

[Modern Cartooning — publisher page](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/214645/modern-cartooning-by-christopher-hart/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The downside is that Hart's books can sometimes teach you *his particular cartoon vocabulary* rather than giving you deeper drawing fundamentals.

So I'd use it as:

**"How do I turn my drawing skills into cartooning?"**

rather than:

**"How do I learn drawing from scratch?"**

#### 3. **Basic Drawing Made Amazingly Easy - Christopher Hart**

This one is particularly relevant to what you said about wanting to create **abstract concept drawings and then refine them**.

It starts with five basic shapes - circle, oval, square, cylinder and rectangle - and then introduces line, mass, perspective, light and shading before moving toward more complex subjects. ([PenguinRandomhouse.com](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/209413/basic-drawing-made-amazingly-easy-by-christopher-hart/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

[Basic Drawing Made Amazingly Easy — publisher page](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/209413/basic-drawing-made-amazingly-easy-by-christopher-hart/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

You don't necessarily need all the shading/rendering material for a South Park-esque style. But the **shape → form → object** progression is very useful.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

#### 4. **Keys to Drawing - Bert Dodson**

This is the one I'd recommend if you discover that your biggest problem isn't *"I don't know how to draw cartoons"* but rather:

> **"I can imagine what I want, but I can't get it onto paper accurately."**

Dodson focuses heavily on **seeing, proportion, contour, negative space and translating what you see into marks**. It's frequently recommended as a beginner/self-study drawing book. ([Art of Sketching](https://artofsketching.com/drawing-books/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

It isn't particularly cartoon-oriented, but that's actually useful.

You could use it to develop your underlying drawing ability while **deliberately keeping your output stylized**.

### One book I would *not* make your first choice

**Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain - Betty Edwards** is a famous beginner recommendation, and its exercises around observation, contours and negative space can be useful. ([unschooled.art](https://www.unschooled.art/art-education/best-drawing-books-for-beginners/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

But for **your** objective, I'd put *Fun with a Pencil* ahead of it.

You're not primarily trying to become good at reproducing what is in front of you. You're trying to develop a pipeline like:

**idea → rough visual abstraction → recognizable shapes → character/object construction → simplified cartoon → clean lines → flat colors**

So construction and simplification are particularly valuable.

## If I were learning exactly what you're describing

I'd actually use **two books rather than trying to find one perfect book**:

### Phase 1 - learn to construct things

**Andrew Loomis - *Fun with a Pencil***

Learn:

`circle / box / cylinder → 3D form → head/body → pose → character`

Don't worry about making beautiful drawings.

Your exercises could literally be:

- draw 20 heads using spheres
- draw 20 bodies using boxes
- draw 20 characters using only circles + rectangles
- draw the same character from 5 angles
- exaggerate each character
- reduce each character to 3-5 major shapes

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Phase 2 - learn cartoon language

**Christopher Hart - *Modern Cartooning***

Now take your construction knowledge and learn:

`construction → personality → expression → pose → character`

This is much closer to the kind of thing you're describing.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Phase 3 - develop your own visual language

This part actually **shouldn't come from a book**.

I'd give yourself exercises such as:

**Exercise A - abstraction**

Pick something complicated:

> "angry businessman holding an umbrella during a storm"

Draw it in:

1.  30 seconds
2.  2 minutes
3.  5 minutes
4.  15 minutes

But each version must become **simpler**, not more detailed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Exercise B - shape vocabulary**

Draw an object using only:

- circles
- rectangles
- triangles
- straight lines

Then redraw it using **only curved lines**.

Then reduce it until you could recognize it as a tiny icon.

That's extremely relevant to your eventual style.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Exercise C - concept → cartoon**

Write:

> "a person who is terrified of a tiny mouse"

Don't look at references.

Make a terrible little stick-figure sketch communicating the idea.

Then ask:

**What are the 3-5 visual things that make this idea readable?**

Maybe:

- huge eyes
- tiny mouse
- body leaning backwards
- raised hands
- open mouth

Then turn those into a clean cartoon.

That's essentially **visual storytelling through abstraction**, which is much closer to your goal than traditional "learn to draw a realistic apple" exercises.

## One more book worth knowing about

**The Humongous Book of Cartooning - Christopher Hart** is basically the expanded version of the cartooning approach. It covers characters, animals, layouts, backgrounds, composition, character design, etc., and is explicitly designed as a broad beginner cartooning reference. ([PenguinRandomhouse.com](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/76235/humongous-book-of-cartooning-by-christopher-hart/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))
