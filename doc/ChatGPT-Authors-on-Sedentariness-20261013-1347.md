Yes. There is a fascinating intellectual tradition that treats **sedentariness, the domestication of plants and animals, and the rise of settled agricultural societies as foundational mistakes in human history**—or at least as developments that introduced new forms of domination, inequality, disease, and state control.

James C. Scott's *Against the Grain* is an excellent starting point, especially because it challenges the conventional story that agriculture and permanent settlement were straightforward steps toward human progress.

One important distinction: most of these authors don't literally call sedentariness the “root of all evil.” Rather, they argue that settling down, cultivating crops, accumulating surpluses, or building states created the conditions for many of the problems we associate with civilization.

Here are the authors I'd recommend, starting with those closest to Scott's argument.

## 4. A useful way to distinguish these authors

| Author | What is the fundamental problem? |
|----|----|
| James C. Scott | Sedentary grain agriculture enables states, taxation, and coercion |
| Jared Diamond | Agriculture often worsened health and increased inequality |
| Richard Manning | Agriculture reorganizes human society and ecosystems around production |
| Marshall Sahlins | The assumption that foraging life was defined by scarcity |
| John Zerzan | Civilization, domestication, and the structures of mediated existence |
| Graeber and Wengrow | The assumption that settlement and agriculture inevitably produce hierarchy |
| Daniel Quinn | The civilizational worldview of control, production, and expansion |

One final distinction is worth keeping in mind: **sedentariness and agriculture are not identical.** Some hunter-gatherers lived in relatively permanent settlements, while some agriculturalists remained mobile. Scott's argument becomes especially interesting because he examines the particular combination of sedentism, cereal agriculture, population concentration, and state power—not just the act of living in one place.

That makes the question more precise: *Was settling down itself the problem, or was it the particular social and political system that developed around settled agricultural life?* The authors above offer very different answers.
