Yes. What you're describing has a surprisingly long intellectual history, and I think the most interesting authors aren't necessarily the usual "redpill" writers. They're thinkers who question whether **ordinary life is a system for dissipating human potential while preventing people from pursuing genuinely transformative goals.**

Your quotation contains several distinct ideas:

- **The opportunity-cost thesis:** People spend their limited time, intelligence, and energy on reproduction, pets, entertainment, consumer choices, and personal hobbies instead of pursuing something of much greater consequence.
- **The false-fulfillment thesis:** Society teaches people to find meaning in activities that keep them occupied, comfortable, and manageable rather than helping them achieve anything extraordinary.
- **The hidden-purpose thesis:** Activities that could fundamentally transform society are neglected, suppressed, or criminalized, while relatively harmless distractions are encouraged.
- **The civilizational thesis:** Humanity could create something resembling paradise on Earth, but its habits, institutions, and priorities prevent it from doing so.

These ideas aren't identical. Some authors believe ordinary life is intrinsically empty; others believe society actively prevents human flourishing; still others argue that the pursuit of paradise itself can become dangerous.

Here are the thinkers I'd investigate first.

## 4. What about the idea that the meaningful activities are actively suppressed?

This is a slightly different thesis from the claim that ordinary life is distracting. It suggests that **the activities most capable of transforming society are systematically marginalized or prohibited.**

Three further authors are useful here:

- **<Entity value="Michel Foucault" category="people"/> - *<Entity value="Discipline and Punish" category="book"/>* (1975).** Examines how institutions shape behavior, classify people, enforce norms, and define what counts as acceptable conduct. - **<Entity value="Murray Bookchin" category="people"/> - *<Entity value="The Ecology of Freedom" category="book"/>* (1982).** Explores the historical emergence of hierarchy and domination, and considers how ecological and social arrangements might be reorganized around freedom and cooperation.
- **<Entity value="Jacques Camatte" category="people"/> - *<Entity value="The Wandering of Humanity" category="book"/>* (1973).** Offers a particularly radical critique of how capitalism expands beyond the workplace and comes to organize human life itself. Relevant if you suspect that even rebellion, leisure, and personal identity can be absorbed into the system being resisted.

One caution: these writers provide frameworks for analyzing institutional control, but their arguments do not establish that a very small number of genuinely world-transformative activities have been deliberately criminalized. That stronger proposition needs specific historical or empirical evidence.

I think I understand the structure of your hypothesis, and it connects several traditions of political theory, sociology, anthropology, and critiques of capitalism that are often discussed separately.

Your interpretation is more specific than the familiar claim that authoritarian parents produce authoritarian children. You're proposing a theory of **social fragmentation**:

- Small, isolated households cannot provide everything people need for economic security, social belonging, education, collective action, and mutual aid.
- The weakening of extended kinship networks and local communities makes people more dependent on large institutions.
- Those institutions offer substitute forms of belonging-national identity, the corporation, the school, the party, the state-that can channel people's loyalties toward centralized power.
- Competition, isolation, and conflict between households make it harder for people to organize horizontally and exercise collective autonomy.

In this account, the key problem isn't simply that families are authoritarian. It's that **a society organized around isolated nuclear households may be structurally dependent on institutions much larger than those households, and those institutions can exploit that dependency.**

There are authors who have explored substantial parts of this argument. Some go further and explicitly analyze the deliberate destruction of local solidarities as a strategy of domination.

One historical distinction is worth making at the outset: Horkheimer's 1936 essay *Authority and the Family* examines the family's role in reproducing authority, obedience, and social character. Your interpretation of the nuclear family as a deliberately undersized unit that creates demand for centralized institutions is a further theoretical hypothesis, not something we can automatically attribute to Horkheimer. It deserves to be investigated on its own terms.

## 8. What about children being taken from families?

Here I would separate the theoretical question from the historical evidence.

There are documented cases in which governments deliberately separated children from their families and communities as part of assimilationist or colonial policies. For example, Australia's *Bringing Them Home* inquiry documented the forced removal of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children, including removals intended to advance assimilation. \<Cite refs={\["turn822547search3","turn822547search10"\]}/\>

That history is directly relevant to your concern about the destruction of community continuity. In these cases, separation from parents could also mean separation from language, culture, kinship networks, and collective identity.

But this does not establish that compulsory public education everywhere has the same purpose. Schooling can also provide literacy, knowledge, social opportunities, and protection from abusive households. The question worth investigating is which institutional arrangements support children's and communities' autonomy, and which undermine it.

## 10. Why *Irre!* may feel too superficial to you

I think the distinction is that Manfred Lütz's book is principally a popular introduction to psychiatry and psychotherapy, using the contrast between "normal" and "abnormal" people to challenge stigma and rigid social expectations. The deeper question is:

**What social arrangements make people dependent on being considered normal in the first place?**

That question brings together political economy, anthropology, social psychology, and institutional theory. It asks not merely why people conform, but what happens to their alternatives when they lack independent networks of support.

And I'd make one further distinction. Three claims need to be investigated separately:

1.  Modern economic and political arrangements can weaken independent communities.
2.  People who lack meaningful belonging may become more dependent on mainstream recognition and institutional identities.
3.  Powerful institutions deliberately maintain this fragmentation because organized communities threaten their control.

The first two have substantial bodies of relevant scholarship behind them. The third is a stronger claim about intention and coordination; it requires specific historical evidence rather than being inferred from the existence of fragmentation alone.
