Absolutely. *The Matrix* is a particularly good example because it expresses your idea on several levels at once: people are born into a system they don't understand, they mistake its rules for reality, they defend the very structure that enslaves them, and escaping requires not just courage but a fundamental transformation of how they perceive the world.

There is a whole tradition of books and films built around this idea. Some portray literal enslavement; others explore psychological, social, political, or spiritual bondage. The most interesting ones ask a deeper question:

**What if the prison is so effective that most people don't even recognize it as a prison-and freedom initially feels more frightening than captivity?**

## One final thought

I think there are actually three distinct ideas in what you're describing:

1.  **Inherited captivity:** We enter a world whose rules and assumptions are already established. We don't choose the framework in which we learn to think.
2.  **Normalized captivity:** Because everyone around us accepts those rules, we mistake what is familiar for what is natural, necessary, or even desirable.
3.  **The cost of liberation:** Escaping requires questioning deeply rooted beliefs, enduring uncertainty, and taking responsibility for a life no longer dictated by the familiar system.

The third point is especially important. In many of these stories, the decisive obstacle isn't a locked door. It's the possibility that, once the door opens, you might discover that you no longer know how to live.

**I'd be curious about one distinction:** when you say "slavery," are you thinking primarily about the way modern society conditions people to work, consume, and conform-or something more existential, such as being enslaved by one's own desires, fears, beliefs, and unconscious habits?

Both interpretations fit your description, but they lead to some rather different-and fascinating-books.
