Yes - and there’s actually a fairly rich literature that matches what you’re describing. The important distinction is between **clinical/research-oriented books**, **first-person therapeutic accounts**, and **anthologies of psychedelic experiences**. For your particular interest-*“what are the kinds of profound positive changes people report after these substances?”*-I’d prioritize the latter two.

### Closest to what you're looking for

**1. *My Psychedelic Explorations* - Claudio Naranjo**

Probably my first recommendation for you.

Naranjo was a psychiatrist/psychotherapist and one of the early investigators of psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy. The book is essentially a **psychedelic autobiography mixed with clinical material**: accounts of sessions, interviews, research papers, and his work with **MDMA, ayahuasca, iboga, psilocybin, cannabis, LSD**, etc. It specifically discusses therapeutic and transformational effects rather than merely describing recreational experiences. ([Simon & Schuster](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/My-Psychedelic-Explorations/Claudio-Naranjo/9781644110584?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**2. *Psychedelics and Psychotherapy: The Healing Potential of Expanded States* - Tim Read & Maria Papaspyrou (eds.)**

This is probably the best **collection** for your purposes. ### If you specifically want a *collection of trip stories*

There's a book almost comically close to your wording:

***Tripping: An Anthology of True-Life Psychedelic Adventures***, edited by Charles Hayes.

It's a collection of first-person psychedelic experiences rather than a clinical textbook. It covers the experiential side-the strange, transformative, beautiful, frightening, hilarious, and occasionally therapeutic things people report. A contemporary review describes it as a 448-page anthology of true-life psychedelic adventures. ([SFGate](https://www.sfgate.com/books/article/Good-Trips-Bad-Trips-the-Use-and-Abuse-of-2693213.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That's probably worth seeking out if your primary interest is **“give me hundreds of people's accounts of what happened to them.”**

Another one worth knowing is:

***The Psychedelic Explorer's Guide* - James Fadiman**

This is more of a synthesis of psychedelic experience and practical psychedelic psychology, but Fadiman incorporates a substantial number of **case reports and anecdotal accounts**. It's especially interesting for the idea that people sometimes report unexpectedly specific changes after psychedelic experiences.

### If you're interested in the *old clinical case-report tradition*

This rabbit hole gets really interesting.

***LSD Psychotherapy* - Stanislav Grof**

Grof's work is essentially a giant archive of observations from thousands of psychedelic sessions. It's much more specifically about LSD-assisted psychotherapy than about recreational psychedelic use, and it contains extensive descriptions of what people experienced psychologically and how those experiences related to trauma, memories, personality changes, existential issues, etc.

It's less “fun trip reports” and more:

> *Patient presents with X → psychedelic session produces Y → subsequent psychological changes occur.*

So if what fascinates you about the Stamets story is the **unexpected therapeutic transformation**, Grof is very much worth reading.

### There's also an interesting older literature

***Timothy Leary: The Harvard Years***, edited by James Penner, collects the early Harvard-era research on **psilocybin and LSD**, including the Concord Prison Project and Good Friday Experiment. It contains original research papers and writings concerning therapeutic, spiritual and religious effects. ([Simon & Schuster](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Timothy-Leary-The-Harvard-Years/James-Penner/9781620552353?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### One fascinating thing about the Stamets example

The basic anecdote you remember is real: Stamets has publicly described his first psilocybin experience as dramatically affecting his lifelong stutter. ([Paul Stamets](https://paulstamets.com/news/tim-ferriss-show-interview-?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

What's especially interesting is that **there is now actually some preliminary research specifically on psychedelics and stuttering**. A 2024 qualitative study examined 114 self-reported experiences from people who stutter and use classic psychedelics; 74% reported positive overall short-term effects, frequently involving reduced stuttering and anxiety. The authors emphasize, however, that this is uncontrolled self-report data and absolutely isn't evidence that psychedelics treat stuttering. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38833909/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That is a perfect example of the distinction I'd keep in mind while reading these books:

**anecdote → intriguing hypothesis → controlled investigation → established treatment**

There are *many* extraordinary anecdotes in psychedelic literature, but the evidentiary strength varies enormously.
